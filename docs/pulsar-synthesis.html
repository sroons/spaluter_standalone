Pulsar synthesis · an overview for players

A stream of tiny sounds.

Most synthesizers start with a continuous tone and carve it. Pulsar synthesis starts with almost nothing: one very short sound, repeated. Everything you hear comes from how fast it repeats, how long each one lasts, what is inside it, and which repeats you leave out.

Where it comes from

Named after a star.

A pulsar is a collapsed star that spins and sweeps a beam past the Earth at a perfectly regular rate, anywhere from once every few seconds to hundreds of times a second. Composer Curtis Roads borrowed the name for a technique he developed in the 1990s and described in his book Microsound (2001): music made from regular trains of sound particles.

The idea has older roots. In the electronic music studios of the 1950s, composers fed the clicks of laboratory pulse generators through filters and found that a click train could be a rhythm, a pitch, or a tone colour depending on its speed and on the filter. Pulsar synthesis takes that discovery, removes the laboratory, and gives each element its own control.

Roads and Alberto de Campo wrote the first dedicated program, PulsarGenerator, and Roads used it for pieces such as Half-life and the works collected on Point Line Cloud. Spiluter puts the same technique in a box you can play.

The building block

Pulsaret, then silence.

One pulsar has two parts: a short burst of waveform called the pulsaret, followed by a gap of silence. Repeat that over and over and you have a pulsar train.

Two timings describe it. How often a new pulsar begins is the pulse rate. How long the pulsaret lasts inside each one is the duty. They are independent, and that independence is the whole instrument.

Orange: the sound. Blue dashes: the envelope, called the window, that fades each pulsaret in and out.

Idea one

Rhythm and pitch are the same fader.

A pulsar train has no fixed identity. Slow, it is a rhythm. Fast, it is a pitch. In between is a zone that is neither, and pulsar music spends a lot of time crossing it on purpose. On Spiluter this is Base Pitch, or the note you play.

1 – 8
Pulses per second

Separate events you can count. A clock, a drip, a slow motor.

8 – 20
Flutter

Too fast to count, not yet a note. Rolls, purring, a card in bicycle spokes.

20 – 200
Low pitch

The ticks fuse into a buzzing bass tone whose colour comes from the formant.

200 +
Pitch

A clear note. Now the pulsaret and its window decide whether it is soft, nasal or bright.

One clock · three formants

Three streams share one pulse rate. Each has a different number of wiggles inside the pulsaret: a different formant.

Idea two

The formant is a second pitch.

Inside each pulsaret the waveform has its own speed. That speed is heard as a formant: a band of emphasis in the tone, like the resonance that turns a sung "oo" into an "ee". Change it and the note stays put while its colour slides.

Because the formant does not depend on the pulse rate, you can hold a note and sweep the vowel, or hold the vowel and play a melody under it. With a filter you would need the filter to track the keyboard; here it is simply how the sound is built.

Spiluter runs up to three formants at once on every voice, each panned separately, so one note can be a wide, three-part chord of resonances. Track Pitch makes them follow your playing. Quantize pins the second and third to musical intervals of the first.

Idea three

The particle has a shape.

Two things decide the character of each particle. The pulsaret is the raw waveform: a sine is pure and round, a saw or square is buzzy, noise is breathy. Spiluter has ten and its Pulsaret fader blends from each one into the next.

The window is the envelope laid over it. A soft, rounded window gives smooth, flute-like or vocal tones. A sharp window, or one that starts hard and decays, gives percussive, plucked, glassy ones. Often the window changes the sound more than the waveform does.

Duty sets how much of each period the particle fills. Short duty: thin, bright, lots of air between pulses. Long duty: full and dense. Set Duty Mode to Formant and the instrument does it the classic way, letting the formant decide the length of each particle.

Same waveform · four windows

Soft, round, pluck, hard. Blue is the window, orange is what comes out.

Masking leaves pulses out, in a pattern or by chance.

Idea four

Leave some out.

Masking silences some of the pulses. It is the part of pulsar synthesis with no real equivalent on an ordinary synth.

Burst masking repeats a pattern, such as three pulses on and one off. At rhythm speeds that is a groove. At pitch speeds the pattern itself becomes audible as a deeper tone under the note, so changing two small numbers moves the sound down by musical intervals.

Stochastic masking drops pulses at random. A little adds grit and life. A lot breaks a tone into crackle, rain and sputter. With Per-Formant on, each of the three formants is masked separately, and the sound flickers between its resonances across the stereo field.

On the instrument

The words on the screen.

Pulsaret
The waveform inside each particle. Ten shapes, blended by one fader.
Window
The envelope over each particle. Soft windows sing, sharp ones click.
Duty
How much of each pulse period is sound. The rest is silence.
Formant 1 · 2 · 3
The resonances: the "vowel" of the sound. Independent of the note.
Base pitch
The pulse rate. Low values are rhythm, higher ones are notes.
Mask
Leaves pulses out, in a burst pattern or at random.
Amp jitter
Random loudness from one particle to the next. Adds grain and breath.
Timing jitter
Nudges each particle early or late. Loosens a rigid tone into something organic.
Glisson
Bends the pitch inside every particle, up or down. Chirps, zaps and liquid attacks.
Sample
Use a recording of your own as the particle in place of a built-in waveform.
Going further

Read and listen.

Spiluter's direction is inspired by Curtis Roads' work on granular and microsound composition.

  • Microsound, Curtis Roads (MIT Press, 2001). The book that sets out pulsar synthesis alongside the other particle techniques. MIT Press
  • Sound Composition with Pulsars, Curtis Roads (Journal of the Audio Engineering Society, 2001). The technique in one article, with its history.
  • The Computer Music Tutorial, second edition, Curtis Roads (MIT Press, 2023). The wider background. MIT Press
  • Point Line Cloud, Curtis Roads (2004). An album of pieces made with these techniques, including Half-life. The best way to hear what the method can do.
